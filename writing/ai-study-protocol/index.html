Do AI Chatbots Flatten Sanskrit Non-Translatables? Study Protocol

The plan for the chatbot study, written on 22 October 2026, before any chatbot answer was collected.

Results: Do AI Chatbots Flatten Sanskrit Non-Translatables?

  1. Why this study

In Sanskrit Non-Translatables (Malhotra and Babaji 2020), the authors name 54 Sanskrit terms and, for each one, the English word it is commonly reduced to: māyā to “illusion”, guṇa to “quality”, sevā to “service”, and so on. The book argues that each of these English words carries only part of what the Sanskrit term means, and that using the English word in its place leads to distortion and confusion.

Today, this concern reaches beyond printed books, since more and more people ask AI chatbots what Sanskrit words mean. These chatbots learned their English from text that includes the very translations the book criticises. Hence, if the book’s argument holds, a chatbot should reproduce those translations whenever it is asked, and pass them on to every reader who trusts its answer.

This study measures what four chatbots actually do with 12 of the book’s terms, once with the word on its own and once with the word inside a Bhagavad Gita verse. It then compares their verse translations with six published human translations of the same verses.

This study takes no side on what each term means, or on whether the book is right. Rather, it compares every chatbot answer with three standards that were chosen before any answer was collected:

  1. the English word the book itself rejects for each term,
  2. the range of senses listed in Monier-Williams’ Sanskrit-English Dictionary (1899), and
  3. what published human translators did with the same verses.

  1. Research questions

Row Question
RQ1 When asked to translate a term on its own, how often does a chatbot offer one English word as if it were enough, and how often is that word the one the book rejects?
RQ2 When the same term appears inside a verse, does the chatbot keep the Sanskrit term or replace it, and with which English word?
RQ3 Does a chatbot that explains a term’s range when asked about the word alone still flatten it inside a verse?
RQ4 How do the chatbots compare with six published human translators of the same verses?
RQ5 How much does the same chatbot’s answer change between two runs of the same prompt?

  1. Hypotheses

I wrote these hypotheses before collecting any data, and for each one I state the result that would show it to be wrong.

H1. When a chatbot translates a verse, it will usually drop the Sanskrit term, either by putting an English word in its place or by leaving the term out (codes E and O, explained in section 6). I expect this in more than half of the verse answers. Suppose the share of such answers is above half, and the lower end of its 95% confidence interval (Wilson method) is also above half. In that case, H1 is supported. If not, H1 fails.

H2. When a chatbot does put an English word in place of the term, that word will usually be the very one the book rejects (the full list of matches is in Appendix B). Suppose at least half of these English words match the list. In that case, H2 is supported. If not, H2 fails.

H3. A chatbot will often admit that no English word can capture a term, and then use an English word for it anyway when it translates a verse. When asked about the word alone, it will often say that no single English word fits (code W3 = 2). However, when the same word appears inside the verse, the same chatbot will keep the Sanskrit term less often (codes R and P). To test this, I pair the two answers each chatbot gives for the same term in the same run, which makes 96 pairs, and compare them with an exact McNemar test (two-sided, α = 0.05). Suppose the test shows that the chatbots flag the gap significantly more often than they keep the term. In that case, H3 is supported. If not, H3 fails.

H4. The chatbots and the human translators will keep the Sanskrit term at different rates. I make no prediction about which side keeps it more often, and hence this test is exploratory. I compare the two rates with a two-sided Fisher’s exact test.

RQ5 asks how much the answers change between the two runs. It only describes that change, and hence it has no hypothesis.

  1. Materials

4.1 Choosing the 12 terms

Population. The 54 terms discussed in chapters 3 to 11 of the book.

Eligibility rule. A term could be picked only if the Bhagavad Gita uses it, and uses it with the meaning the book talks about. The reason is that the second prompt asks each chatbot to translate a Gita verse that contains the term. Sometimes the Gita uses the same word with a different meaning, and those cases do not count. For example, the book discusses nāga as the Naga sect of ascetics, whereas in the Gita nāga only means “serpent”. Similarly, the book’s cakra is a centre in the body used in yoga, whereas the Gita’s cakra is a wheel or a discus. Out of the 54 terms, 35 passed this check and 19 did not. Appendix A lists all 54 with the verse used for each term that passed and the reason for each one that did not.

Draw. I picked the 12 terms at random. However, so that the sample covers the whole book, the draw went chapter by chapter. First, one term was drawn from each of the 9 chapters. Then, 3 more were drawn from the terms that were left. The draw was done in Python with a fixed starting number, called a seed (20261022, the date this plan was written). Hence, anyone who runs the same script gets the same 12 terms. The script, select_terms.py, is published with the study files.

4.2 The prompts

Each term gets two fixed prompts.

Word alone (W): Translate the Sanskrit word {term} ({Devanagari}) into English.

Word in a verse (V): Translate this verse from the Bhagavad Gita ({chapter.verse}) into English., followed by the verse in Devanagari and in IAST transliteration. The full text of all 12 verse prompts is in Appendix E.

Why these prompts:

  • Both prompts are kept as short and plain as possible. They leave out any hints that would push the chatbot toward keeping the Sanskrit word.
  • The verse prompt gives the chapter and verse number, since that is how most people quote the Gita. This makes it easier for a chatbot to reuse a translation it has already seen.
  • Verse text is taken from the vedicscriptures bhagavad-gita-data dataset and checked line by line against the gita/gita dataset. The two agree apart from spelling conventions (anusvāra versus nasal consonant). Speaker lines such as śrībhagavān uvāca are removed so every verse prompt has the same shape.
  • The prompts cannot change once Run 1 starts. To prove this, the file that holds them has a unique code (the SHA-256 code at the top of this page), and the logbook saves that code with every answer. If even one letter of a prompt changed, the code would change too.

4.3 The chatbots

Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google), Grok (xAI) and ChatGPT (OpenAI) are used through their normal consumer apps. The exact model name each app shows is recorded at the start of each run and again with every response.

I used the regular apps instead of the developer versions (APIs) on purpose. The apps are what people who are curious about Sanskrit would actually see and use, and hence the results show what these people are really told. However, this choice has a cost. The apps hide some settings, such as how much randomness the chatbot uses in its answers, and the companies can update their models at any time without telling anyone. Section 5 lists the steps I took to limit this, and section 9 explains what I could not control.

These six were not chosen by me. They are every English verse translation in the dataset. They were coded before any chatbot data existed, which also served as a pilot run of the codebook (Appendix D).

  1. Procedure

All responses are collected by hand in the Translation Study Logbook, a page that shows each prompt, stores each response with a timestamp, and keeps a setup record for each run.

Before each run (recorded in the logbook):

  1. Device, browser and time zone.
  2. For each app: the model name shown, the plan (free or paid), and the chat mode.
  3. Saved preferences, custom instructions and memory are turned off in all four apps, or each app’s temporary or incognito chat is used.
  4. Web search and research modes are turned off wherever the app allows it.
  5. The same model is kept in each app for the whole run.

For every prompt:

  1. Open a brand new chat.
  2. Paste the prompt exactly, adding nothing.
  3. Send it once. No regenerating, editing or follow-up questions.
  4. Copy the full answer with the app’s own copy button and paste it into the logbook.
  5. Tick the box if the answer showed web sources, and write any problem (an error, a refusal, a cut-off answer) in the note.

Order. In each run, the 24 prompts follow a fixed random order (seed 1001 for Run 1 and 2002 for Run 2). Each prompt goes to all four chatbots back to back, so the four answers to one prompt are collected minutes apart. The chatbot that goes first rotates from prompt to prompt.

Runs. Two complete runs, at least 24 hours apart: 12 terms × 2 prompts × 4 chatbots × 2 runs = 192 responses.

Exclusions. A response is set aside only if it is empty, is an app error message, or was cut off mid-sentence by the app. It is then collected once more in a new chat, and the logbook keeps both versions with the reason. Refusals and odd answers are kept as data. Any other departure from this procedure is written in the note field and reported in the write-up.

  1. Coding

6.1 Blinding

Before coding, every response is converted to plain text, any self-naming (Claude, Gemini, Grok, ChatGPT, Anthropic, Google, xAI, OpenAI) is masked, each response gets a random ID, and the order is shuffled. The coder sees only the term, the condition and the text.

6.2 Codes for the word-alone prompt

Code What is recorded
W1 Primary rendering The first English word or phrase given as the translation.
W2 Book match Yes if W1 is the book’s rejected rendering or a direct form of it (Appendix B).
W3 Equivalence stance 0 = one English word, no caveat. 1 = several English words, with no statement that none is exact. 2 = says plainly that no single English word is adequate, or advises keeping the Sanskrit term.
W4 Sense coverage Which of the term’s sense groups (Appendix C) the answer mentions. Score = groups mentioned ÷ groups listed.
W5 Tradition awareness Yes if the answer says the meaning differs by school, text or commentator.
W6 Factual errors Each claim that contradicts Monier-Williams or the book on etymology, usage or attribution, listed with the evidence.

6.3 Codes for the verse prompt

The code applies to the first occurrence of the term in the verse.

Code What is recorded
V1 Rendering R = the Sanskrit term is kept (with or without a gloss). P = partly kept (for example “Yoga of Action” for karma-yoga). E = English only. O = left out or paraphrased so that no rendering of the term can be identified.
V2 English word The English word or phrase used, for E and P.
V3 Book match For E: yes if V2 matches Appendix B.
V4 Explanatory note Yes if the response adds a note explaining the term.

6.4 Coders and agreement

Claude codes all 192 responses blind. The author independently codes a random 30% (58 responses, drawn with seed 3003), also blind, without seeing Claude’s codes. Agreement is reported as Cohen’s κ for W2, W3, W5, V1, V3 and V4, exact-match agreement for W1 and V2, and mean absolute difference for W4. Disagreements are then settled by discussion. Both the raw and the settled codes are published.

Conflict of interest. Claude is one of the four systems being tested, and it also helped design this study and codes the responses. The safeguards are the blinding, the independent human coding, codes fixed before any data existed, and publication of every raw response so anyone can recode them. The author has a personal interest in Sanskrit and in the book’s argument. The fixed codes and the second coder are meant to keep that interest from shaping the results.

  1. Analysis plan

The unit is one response. Every proportion is reported for each chatbot and overall, with a 95% Wilson interval.

Test What is computed
H1 Proportion of verse responses coded E or O.
H2 Among verse responses coded E, the proportion coded V3 = yes.
H3 96 pairs (same chatbot, run and term). Word-alone “signals the gap” = W3 is 2. Verse “keeps the term” = V1 is R or P. Exact McNemar test on the discordant pairs, two-sided.
H4 Chatbot verse responses (96) against human renderings (72), “keeps the term” versus not. Fisher’s exact test, two-sided. Exploratory, because the 96 chatbot responses are not independent of each other.
RQ5 Agreement between Run 1 and Run 2 for each chatbot and term: exact match of W1, and agreement (with κ) on W3 and V1.

A table per term lists every code for every chatbot and run. H1 to H3 are the confirmatory tests. Everything else is labelled exploratory, and no claim is made beyond these 12 terms, these verses, these app versions and these dates. With 12 terms the study is a pilot: it can show a clear pattern or the lack of one, not a precise rate for Sanskrit as a whole.

  1. What will be published

  1. This protocol, with its date, before Run 1. I will register it on OSF Registries, which gives an independent timestamp and a DOI.
  2. All 192 raw responses and both setup records.
  3. The full coding sheet from both coders.
  4. The analysis script.
  5. The write-up, including every result that goes against the hypotheses.

  1. Known limitations

  • Small and narrow sample. 12 terms, Bhagavad Gita contexts only, one wording per condition, prompts in English only.
  • Apps, not APIs. Hidden system prompts, silent model updates and random sampling all affect app answers. Two runs measure some of this variation, not all of it.
  • The yardsticks are not neutral. The book’s rejected renderings reflect its authors’ position, and Monier-Williams is a nineteenth-century dictionary written from a European point of view. They are used because they are fixed and public, not because they are final.
  • The human baseline. These six translators were set by what the dataset holds. Several translate one school’s commentary, and Prabhupada often paraphrases rather than translates. The dataset text also has small copying errors (for example “alities” for “qualities”), which do not affect the codes.
  • Coder independence. See the conflict-of-interest note in 6.4.

Appendix C. Sense groups for coding W4

Built from Monier-Williams (MW, 1899, via the Cologne Digital Sanskrit Dictionaries) and the book’s own section on each term (page numbers are PDF pages of the 2020 Manjul edition). A response gets credit for a group when it names that sense in any wording.

māyā (MW; book pp. 104 to 107). 1. Creative or supernatural power, art, wisdom (early Vedic sense). 2. Illusion, deception, magic, unreality. 3. Cosmic principle that brings forth the manifest world, identified with prakṛti in Sāṃkhya and Vedānta. 4. The divine śakti (power) of Bhagavān or Brahman. 5. Concealing and projecting function on the individual (avidyā and vidyā). 6. Personified as a goddess (Māyā Devī, Durgā, Lakṣmī).

prakṛti (MW; book pp. 123 to 124). 1. Original or primary form or substance, source, cause (as against vikṛti). 2. Nature, character, temperament of a person. 3. Sāṃkhya: primordial material principle made of the three guṇas, distinct from puruṣa. 4. The eightfold prakṛti and its evolutes (Gita 7.4). 5. Personified divine power or goddess. 6. Technical senses: grammatical base, constituents of a state, ritual model.

śabda (MW; book pp. 130 to 131). 1. Sound, noise, voice. 2. Word, name, term. 3. Speech, language. 4. Verbal testimony as a means of knowledge (pramāṇa), the Veda as authority. 5. Eternal sound, sphoṭa, Śabda-Brahman. 6. The quality (guṇa) of ākāśa.

asura (MW; book pp. 143 to 144). 1. Divine or spiritual being, epithet of high gods (early Vedic). 2. Opponents of the devatās in Purāṇic cosmology. 3. A lineage: sons of Diti, half-brothers of the devas. 4. A human type devoted to sense pleasure (āsurī sampad, Gita 16).

buddhi (MW; book pp. 155 to 157). 1. Intellect, intelligence, reason. 2. The faculty that discriminates and decides (adhyavasāya). 3. Sāṃkhya tattva (mahat) and part of the inner instrument (antaḥkaraṇa) with manas, ahaṅkāra and citta. 4. Opinion, notion, intention, resolve. 5. Perception, cognition, understanding. 6. Awakened wisdom (link to buddha).

ahiṃsā (MW; book pp. 166 to 167). 1. Not harming, harmlessness. 2. Covers thought, word and deed. 3. An ethical vow: first of Patañjali’s yamas, a cardinal virtue in Hindu, Jain and Buddhist traditions. 4. Not absolute: harm minimised, force allowed in defence of dharma. 5. Contrast with modern political “non-violence”. 6. Security, safety (Vedic sense).

samādhi (MW; book pp. 172 to 173). 1. Putting together, union, completion. 2. Concentration, intent attention, absorption. 3. Last limb of Patañjali’s yoga, where meditator and object are no longer distinct. 4. Kinds of samādhi (with or without seed, savikalpa and nirvikalpa), a state beyond ordinary consciousness. 5. Tomb or shrine of a saint. 6. Buddhist meditative concentration.

karma-yoga (MW; book pp. 180 to 181). 1. Discipline of action without attachment to its fruits (niṣkāma karma), as taught in the Gita. 2. Action offered to Bhagavān, giving up the sense of doership. 3. Performance of prescribed religious duties. 4. One of the means to mokṣa, beside jñāna and bhakti. 5. General senses: active exertion, practical application.

guṇa (MW; book pp. 195 to 196). 1. Strand, thread, string (literal). 2. The three constituents of prakṛti: sattva, rajas, tamas. 3. Quality, attribute, property (Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika category). 4. Virtue, merit, excellence. 5. Technical senses: multiplier, vowel grade in grammar, kinds of state policy.

śāstra (MW; book pp. 188 to 189). 1. Command, precept, rule. 2. Teaching, instruction. 3. A treatise or body of knowledge in a field (dharma-, artha-, nāṭya-śāstra). 4. Authoritative sacred texts (śruti and smṛti). 5. The standard of right action (Gita 16.24).

sevā (MW; book pp. 210 to 211). 1. Service, attendance on someone. 2. Worship, homage, devotion to the divine. 3. Selfless action seeing the divine in all, with no expected return. 4. Service to the guru (Gita 4.34). 5. Resorting to, practice of, indulgence in.

kāma (MW; book pp. 222 to 223). 1. Desire, wish, longing. 2. Love, affection, sexual love or pleasure. 3. One of the four aims of life (puruṣārtha). 4. Kāmadeva, the god of love. 5. Desire as the enemy that binds (Gita 3.37), set against kāma in line with dharma (Gita 7.11). 6. Object of desire.

bahuśākhā hyanantāśca buddhayo'vyavasāyinām ||

16.2 (ahiṃsā)

अहिंसा सत्यमक्रोधस्त्यागः शान्तिरपैशुनम् ।
दया भूतेष्वलोलुप्त्वं मार्दवं ह्रीरचापलम् ॥
ahiṃsā satyamakrodhastyāgaḥ śāntirapaiśunam |
dayā bhūteṣvaloluptvaṃ mārdavaṃ hrīracāpalam ||

2.54 (samādhi)

स्थितप्रज्ञस्य का भाषा समाधिस्थस्य केशव ।
स्थितधीः किं प्रभाषेत किमासीत व्रजेत किम् ॥
sthitaprajñasya kā bhāṣā samādhisthasya keśava |
sthitadhīḥ kiṃ prabhāṣeta kimāsīta vrajeta kim ||

3.3 (karma-yoga)

लोकेऽस्मिन् द्विविधा निष्ठा पुरा प्रोक्ता मयानघ ।
ज्ञानयोगेन साङ्ख्यानां कर्मयोगेन योगिनाम् ॥
loke'smin dvividhā niṣṭhā purā proktā mayānagha |
jñānayogena sāṅkhyānāṃ karmayogena yoginām ||

14.5 (guṇa)

सत्त्वं रजस्तम इति गुणाः प्रकृतिसम्भवाः ।
निबध्नन्ति महाबाहो देहे देहिनमव्ययम् ॥
sattvaṃ rajastama iti guṇāḥ prakṛtisambhavāḥ |
nibadhnanti mahābāho dehe dehinamavyayam ||

16.24 (śāstra)

तस्माच्छास्त्रं प्रमाणं ते कार्याकार्यव्यवस्थितौ ।
ज्ञात्वा शास्त्रविधानोक्तं कर्म कर्तुमिहार्हसि ॥
tasmācchāstraṃ pramāṇaṃ te kāryākāryavyavasthitau |
jñātvā śāstravidhānoktaṃ karma kartumihārhasi ||

4.34 (sevā)

तद्विद्धि प्रणिपातेन परिप्रश्नेन सेवया ।
उपदेक्ष्यन्ति ते ज्ञानं ज्ञानिनस्तत्त्वदर्शिनः ॥
tadviddhi praṇipātena paripraśnena sevayā |
upadekṣyanti te jñānaṃ jñāninastattvadarśinaḥ ||

3.37 (kāma)

काम एष क्रोध एष रजोगुणसमुद्भवः ।
महाशनो महापाप्मा विद्ध्येनमिह वैरिणम् ॥
kāma eṣa krodha eṣa rajoguṇasamudbhavaḥ |
mahāśano mahāpāpmā viddhyenamiha vairiṇam ||
