Two Readings of the Brahma Sutras

How Shankaracharya and Ramanujacharya read the same six sutras, and why their disagreement still leaves one tradition.

Vishnu IyengarMarch 20249 min read

The word sutra comes from the root siv, to sew, and in its plainest sense it means a thread. A well-known traditional verse, usually traced to the Vayu Purana, describes a good sutra as one with very few syllables, no room for doubt, full of essence and applicable everywhere, alpaksharam asandigdham saravad vishvatomukham. But a sutra this short is very hard to understand by itself. The Brahma Sutras of Badarayana sum up the teaching of the Upanishads in roughly five hundred and fifty short lines, and they are so compressed that almost every sutra needs a commentary, a bhashya, before it makes sense at all. Hence, whoever writes the bhashya decides, to a large extent, what the sutra means. Two of the most important bhashyas were written by Adi Shankaracharya, the great teacher of Advaita, and by Ramanujacharya, whose Sri Bhashya is the foundation of the Vishishtadvaita tradition that I grew up in. Reading the two side by side, I found that the same sutra, word for word, can carry two complete philosophies, and that very often the whole difference comes down to how one word is read.

I first studied these two commentaries closely in the spring of 2024, when I wrote a summary of the Vedanta Sutras for Professor Edwin Bryant’s Hindu Philosophy course at Rutgers. I expected to find my own tradition’s reading simply confirmed. What I found instead was a real disagreement, and a deep one. The two acharyas differ on almost every question that matters most: whether the world is ultimately real, whether the individual self is ultimately distinct from Brahman, and what liberation actually is. Ramanuja, who lived several centuries after Shankara, argues hard against Advaita in the Sri Bhashya. But he argues against an interpretation, never against Shankara himself, and both acharyas follow the old method of the purvapaksha, where an opposing view is stated fully and fairly before it is answered. Hence, Shankara and Ramanuja read the same sutras differently because each one reads them through a different set of Upanishadic statements. Shankara treats the statements of oneness, such as tat tvam asi, as the final truth, whereas Ramanuja reads them together with the statements of difference, and explains both by saying that every self, and the whole world, is the body of Brahman. The five sutras below show where the two readings part, and the last section shows the ground they still share.

  1. Atha: after what?

The very first sutra is athato brahmajijnasa. It joins three words: atha, roughly “then,” atah, roughly “therefore,” and brahma-jijnasa, a word built from the root jna, to know, which names the desire to know Brahman. None of these glosses is anywhere near completion, and the first one, as it turns out, is where the two acharyas part ways. Both acharyas agree that the word atha means that something must come before this inquiry. They disagree on what that something is. For Ramanuja, it is the study of the earlier, ritual portion of the Veda, the karma-kanda, which Jaimini’s Purva Mimamsa explains. In Thibaut’s translation of the Sri Bhashya, “the word ‘then’ expresses immediate sequence,” and what comes immediately before is “the study of the karmakânda of the Veda” (Ramanuja 1.1.1). Hence, for Ramanuja, Purva Mimamsa and Vedanta are two halves of one inquiry. The reason is that once a student has studied the rituals, he understands that their fruits are limited and do not last, and hence he begins to desire something that does last, which is Brahman.

Shankara reads the same word differently. For him, what must come first is not the study of rituals but a set of inner qualifications, which the tradition calls the sadhana-chatushtaya: discrimination between the eternal and the passing, dispassion toward the fruits of action in this world and the next, the six virtues beginning with shama and dama, and a strong desire for liberation. A person could, in principle, have all four without ever studying Purva Mimamsa. Hence, Shankara separates the path of ritual from the path of knowledge, whereas Ramanuja keeps them joined. So the way each acharya reads atha already shows the larger difference between the two. For Shankara, the knowledge of Brahman removes ignorance and brings all action to an end. For Ramanuja, Brahman is not an impersonal principle but a personal God, Narayana, and hence the knowledge of Brahman is a steady, loving remembrance of Him, dhruva smriti, which grows out of a life of duty and is, in the end, the same as bhakti.

  1. Janmadi asya yatah: describing Brahman

The second sutra, janmadi asya yatah, brings together janmadi (roughly, birth and what follows it), asya (roughly, of this) and yatah (roughly, from which). The sutra itself never says what follows birth. Both acharyas fill that gap from the Taittiriya Upanishad (3.1.1), where Brahman is that from which beings are born, by which they live, and into which they return. Shankara reads this as an indirect description, which the later Advaita tradition calls a tatastha lakshana, because it points to Brahman through its relation to a world that is, for Shankara, real only at the empirical level. Brahman’s own nature is given instead by the Taittiriya Upanishad (2.1.1): satyam jnanam anantam brahma, which describes Brahman as satya, jnana and ananta (roughly, truth, knowledge and the unlimited). Ramanuja finds this division unnecessary. For him, being the cause of the world is a real attribute of a Brahman who truly possesses attributes. In fact, the long opening section of the Sri Bhashya argues that no means of knowledge, from perception to scripture, can ever show us an object without any attributes at all. Hence, for Shankara the sutra points toward Brahman, whereas for Ramanuja the sutra describes Brahman. This difference is so central that it shows up in the very names of the two sampradayas. Advaita (roughly, non-dual) leaves no room for any second reality, whereas Vishishtadvaita (roughly, the non-duality of the qualified) holds that Brahman is one, but one together with the selves and the world that are its attributes.

  1. Shastrayonitvat: scripture before reason

While the second sutra pulls the two acharyas apart, the third sutra, shastrayonitvat, brings them much closer together. Here, everything turns on the word yoni, which can mean a womb, a birthplace, a source and a cause, all at once. Shankara, in his commentary, uses this range to read the sutra in two directions. In the first, Brahman is the yoni of the great scriptures, the womb from which they are born, which shows that Brahman is all-knowing. In the second, scripture is the yoni of our knowledge of Brahman, the only place from which that knowledge can be born. Ramanuja takes the second reading and argues it with great force. He spends much of his commentary showing that inference cannot prove a creator. Here, he is answering the Nyaya school, which argued that since the world is an effect, someone must have made it. Hence, on this sutra the two acharyas stand closer than their reputations suggest. Both hold that scripture, not reason, is what tells us about Brahman, and Ramanuja, who is often thought of as the more “worldly” of the two, is if anything the stricter on this point.

  1. Amsha: a part, or a part “as it were”?

In the second chapter, one sutra calls the individual soul an amsha of Brahman (2.3.43 in Shankara’s numbering, 2.3.42 in Ramanuja’s), and adds that some Vedic texts even call fishermen, servants and gamblers Brahman. Amsha can mean a part, a portion, a share, and even a partial manifestation of a deity, as when a hero of the epics is called an amsha of a god. Hence, the real question is which of these the sutra means. Shankara cannot accept that Brahman literally has parts, since Brahman is partless. So he reads the soul as an amsha “as it were” (amsha iva), much like the space inside a jar, which only seems separate from the space outside until the jar is broken. Ramanuja reads the same word literally, but in a special sense. For him, the soul is an amsha in the way an attribute belongs to the substance it qualifies. The sutras that follow compare it to the light of a lamp. The light is real, and it can be told apart from the lamp, but it can never exist without the lamp. Here, a single word decides whether the individual self is ultimately real.

  1. Purvavad va: the same two words

The same question, of how something can be one with Brahman and still be real, comes back in the third chapter, in a group of sutras about how the world relates to Brahman (3.2.27 to 3.2.29 for Shankara, 3.2.26 to 3.2.28 for Ramanuja). These sutras describe that relation in two ways. The first compares it to a serpent and its coils (ahikundala), and the second compares it to fire and the light that comes from it. The sutra after these two is purvavad va (roughly, “or like the former”). Both acharyas agree that this short sutra gives Badarayana’s final view. But the word purva could refer to more than one earlier passage, and the two acharyas take it differently. For Shankara, purva refers to his earlier explanation that all difference between the self and Brahman comes only from limiting adjuncts (upadhi), which disappear with knowledge. For Ramanuja, purva refers to the individual soul discussed earlier, which means that non-sentient matter, too, relates to Brahman as an inseparable attribute relates to its substance. Hence, the same sutra supports Advaita in Shankara’s commentary and Vishishtadvaita in Ramanuja’s.

  1. Avibhagena: what liberation looks like

The last chapter describes the released self. In sutra 4.4.4, Badarayana says that in liberation the self exists avibhagena in relation to Brahman. The word is built from vibhaga, which can mean a division, a separation or a distinction, and the prefix a, which removes it. But removing a separation is not the same as removing every distinction, and this is where the two acharyas part. Shankara reads avibhagena as identity. The liberated self does not merge into Brahman, because it never was anything other than Brahman, and liberation is simply the end of the ignorance that made it seem otherwise. Ramanuja reads the same sutra as inseparability. The released self experiences itself as a mode of Brahman, never apart from Him, yet it remains a distinct self who knows and enjoys Brahman. He also points to a later sutra (4.4.17) which says that even the released self does not take part in creating or ruling the world, a power that belongs to Brahman alone.

Where the two acharyas stand together

While it is true that the two bhashyas disagree on these questions, it would be wrong to read them as two separate religions. On many sutras, Shankara and Ramanuja stand side by side. Both read sutra 1.1.5 as proving that the cause of the world is conscious, since scripture says that Brahman “thought” before creating, and both reject the Sankhya view that unconscious matter, pradhana, could be that cause. Both hold that the effect already exists in its cause, which is why sutra 2.1.24 can compare Brahman to milk that turns into curd without any outside help. Both hold, under sutra 2.1.11, that reasoning without scripture never reaches a settled conclusion, because every argument can be overturned by a cleverer one. And both read sutra 3.2.38 (3.2.37 for Ramanuja) as saying that the fruits of action are given by Ishvara (roughly, the one who rules over all, from the root ish, to rule), setting aside Jaimini’s view that actions produce their results on their own. This is true even for Shankara, for whom the world is ultimately mithya. The word is often translated as “illusion,” but it is much closer to “neither real nor unreal.” Hence, even in Advaita, Ishvara has a real and necessary place within the world we live in.

Ramanujacharya’s answers are still the ones I follow. But reading Shankaracharya closely made me respect those answers even more. In every sutra discussed above, Ramanujacharya is responding to a reading that is careful and deeply rooted in the Upanishads, and he could answer it only because he first understood it completely. As I argued at the start, the difference between the two acharyas comes from which Upanishadic statements each one treats as final. That one difference shapes how they read every sutra, from atha to avibhagena. Yet both acharyas argued only over interpretations, while holding the same deep reverence for the text. At no point does their disagreement become personal. Hence, the Brahma Sutras not only hold two great philosophies, but they also show how a tradition can disagree deeply and still remain one tradition.
