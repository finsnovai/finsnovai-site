The Constant and the Variable

Reading Sanskrit Non-Translatables as a Student of Samskrtam

Vishnu IyengarJanuary 202613 min read

A translation is a decision. Every time a word moves from one language into another, someone decides which of its meanings will make the journey and which will be left behind. Sanskrit has its own word for this act, anuvada, which, as Nityananda Misra explains in his foreword, “literally means ‘saying again’ or ‘restating’” (Misra). But saying something again is not the same as saying the same thing again. In Sanskrit Non-Translatables: The Importance of Sanskritizing English, Rajiv Malhotra and Satyanarayana Dasa Babaji walk through fifty-four Sanskrit words, from atma and maya to seva and rasa, and show what each one loses when it is said again in English. On the surface, the book reads like a glossary of corrections. Read closely, however, it makes a much larger argument. A Sanskrit word does not carry one meaning but a range of meanings, and translation forces someone to pick one value from that range and throw the rest away. Hence, the real question the book asks is not only whether a word can be translated, but who holds the adhikara, the authority, to decide which meaning survives. The authors say as much in their first chapter, where they describe their whole project as “a means to regain adhikara, the right of dharmic peoples to control the interpretation of knowledge in their own framework” (Malhotra and Babaji, ch. 1).

The idea that a word holds a range of meanings, rather than a single one, is the foundation of the book’s second chapter, and the authors explain it with an image that I, coming from computer science, could not stop thinking about. They write that every word “embodies a spectrum rather than a single value,” and that when such a word “is reduced to only one of its many meanings, it is akin to assigning a specific constant value to an algebraic variable, thereby eliminating its usefulness as a variable” (ch. 2). Their example is the root dih. The root dih begins with the sense of gathering, moves to growing, then to covering, then to concealing, and from concealing it gives deha, the body, which covers the self within. Add the prefix sam, and the root dih becomes sandeha, doubt, a state in which “the consciousness is perfectly clouded, reality is covered, truth is hidden” (ch. 2). Here, the meanings are not a random list. Each meaning grows out of the one before it, like the links of a chain. An English dictionary would list “body” and “doubt” as two unrelated words, whereas Sanskrit shows them as two points on one path of experience. This is what makes a single English equivalent so damaging. It not only drops the other meanings, but it also hides the fact that there was ever a path at all.

Nowhere is this path more visible to me than in names. The authors point out that proper nouns in Sanskrit “are often definite descriptions,” and that the thousand names of Vishnu “capture all the various events relating to him mentioned in the Purana-s” (ch. 2). My own name, Vishnuvallabha, is one such description - the one who is dear to Vishnu. The same care with names appears in the chapter on metaphysics, where the authors observe that Hindus often name their daughters Maya, and add: “It would be absurd to give such names to girls, had maya meant just illusion” (ch. 3). But I was given another name before Vishnuvallabha. At my naming ceremony, my maternal grandfather, a Sanskrit scholar who taught and later served as principal at the Sanskrit college in Melkote, named me Raagneya. I do not know, clearly, what Raagneya means. I have searched for it, and I have not yet found an answer that satisfies me. But what I do know is that he did not choose it at random. Naming is itself a samskara, and the book insists that samskara-s are not empty ceremonies but rites that “give a sense of identity to an individual” (ch. 10). It also explains why a family would choose a name with such care, since a child “is often carefully given an appropriate name so that it will internalize its name as vibration, and over time the effect of repeating the name will bring inner transformation in subtle ways” (ch. 2). Suppose my grandfather had given me a name with one fixed English meaning. In that case, there would have been nothing left to search for. The name, in its untranslated form, is still a variable, and that is exactly what makes it valuable to me.

This idea of a word as a variable did not stay theoretical for me, because I am (at present) a student of Sanskrit myself. I study with Samskrita Bharati, and alongside my classes I built a small website to drill vibhakti, the case endings that a Sanskrit noun must take before it can stand in a sentence. In its section on Shabda, the book says that a better Sanskrit equivalent for the English “word” “would be pada and not Shabda” (ch. 4). For a learner of vyakarana, this is a striking point, because a pada is not a dictionary form at all. Panini defines a pada as that which ends in a sup or tin suffix (Ashtadhyayi 1.4.14), which means a noun becomes a pada only once it has taken its vibhakti. Hence, a Sanskrit word carries its relationship to the rest of the sentence inside itself, something English leaves to word order. While building the site, I made a decision that I understood fully only after reading this book. I kept the drills in Devanagari and allowed English only in the instructions and hints. At the time, I thought of it as a design choice. Now, I see it as the book’s thesis applied on a small scale: the moment a learner reads रामेण as “by Rama,” English takes over, and the vibhakti becomes a translation exercise rather than a way of thinking. The book also reminds the reader of the word for a single letter: “A letter is called akshara, which means ‘imperishable’” (ch. 2). I had already named my larger Sanskrit learning app Akshara, and reading that sentence felt like being given the proper reasoning for a choice I had made by instinct.

The book, as Malhotra makes clear in his preface, is “not meant for teaching Sanskrit” (pref.), and yet some of its most convincing pages are about words that I myself live with on a day-to-day basis. In the chapter on sadhana, the authors write that the purpose of puja “is not perfunctory continuation of a certain tradition, but an expression of prema to establish a relationship with the deity” (ch. 8). When I do puja at home, “ritual” is the last word I would use for it, because a ritual can be performed with no one on the other side, whereas puja is always addressed to someone. The same is true of agni. The Agni of a yajna, the authors explain, “is a deity and is considered the mouth of Bhagavan Himself,” which is why sweets made at home are first offered to Bhagavan through Agni before anyone eats them (ch. 4). The chapter on kavya also speaks to something else I care about deeply, which is Carnatic music. A listener who is qualified to receive a performance, the authors explain, “is called a Sahridaya or a Rasika” (ch. 11). Carnatic audiences are still called rasikas, and no one in a Carnatic concert hall would accept “fans” as a translation, because a rasika is someone trained to receive rasa, not only to hear sound.

If a single English gloss can take over the mind of one learner, it is worth asking what happens when the same gloss is repeated by machines at the scale of millions. Much of my professional work has been in evaluating AI systems, and I see the book’s warning there in a form the authors may not have fully anticipated. A machine translation system, or a large language model, learns what a word means from the company that word keeps in its training data. When it meets maya, it will most likely reach for “illusion,” even though the authors show that maya “denotes the creative Shakti of Brahman or Bhagavan,” and that “even in Advaita Vedanta, maya itself does not stand for illusion but it is the cause of illusion” (ch. 3). Here, the most common translation becomes the only translation, not because anyone argued for it, but because it was repeated the most. To see how far this goes, I tested four AI chatbots on twelve of the book’s fifty-four words. When translating a verse of the Bhagavad Gita, they dropped the Sanskrit word for an English one in 86 of 96 answers, although published human translators had done much the same long before them (Iyengar, “Do AI Chatbots”). The book describes digestion as “the process of chopping up a prey into tiny parts, assimilating the useful portions into the host’s body, and rejecting what is not considered useful” (Malhotra and Babaji, ch. 1). I do not want to stretch the metaphor too far, but the first thing a language model does with any text is, quite literally, to chop it into tiny parts called tokens, and a tokenizer trained mostly on English text tends to break a Devanagari word into far more pieces than an English one. Hence, the risk that the book identifies in scholars and missionaries is now also built into the tools a new generation will use to study Sanskrit for the first time. This is what makes the book more urgent today than when it was published in 2020.

While it is true that a linguist would question some of the book’s claims about sound and meaning, I do not think the book needs those claims to make its case. In the second chapter, the authors argue that sound, meaning and object are bound together from their origin, that the three are “fixed, unlike languages like English,” and that “Sanskrit texts, though written thousands of years ago, still mean the same” (ch. 2). A reader trained in modern linguistics, which treats the link between a sound and its meaning as a convention, may stop at these lines. There is an irony here, however, that the book’s own introduction helps to show. The idea of the arbitrary sign is most closely tied to Ferdinand de Saussure, and Saussure was himself trained as a Sanskritist, a debt to Panini that the authors trace in some detail (introd.). Hence, the very discipline that questions the book’s claim was built, in part, by a reader of Sanskrit grammar. More importantly, the same second chapter also states that meanings “change over time” and that “insisting on fixed, constant, and isolated values is reductionist and distorting” (ch. 2). At first, these two claims seem to pull against each other. However, I read them as working at two different levels. The relation of a Sanskrit word to its root is fixed, and it is precisely this fixed relation that lets its meanings grow in a disciplined way, like the chain of dih, instead of drifting at random. I have tried to test this middle position on the word dharma itself. In a data essay (Iyengar, “How much Dharma?”), I counted the words that stand next to dharma in the Mahabharata, the Ramayana and two dharmashastras. A small core of companions stays with dharma in every text, while the rest change with the context. This is close to the middle position I describe above. Even a reader who does not accept the metaphysics of Shabda-Brahman must still accept the contextual argument, because the chain of dih, the thirty-four names for fire in the Amarakosha, and the many senses of lingam can all be checked in the texts themselves. This is why, when I explain this book to someone skeptical, I would start with what can be verified, and only then move to the deeper claims.

The authors also leave a door open that I find important. In the preface, Malhotra explains that where traditions differ, the book follows the Gaudiya Vaishnava view, and that “we invite collaborations with experts from other dharmic traditions so that the full richness of each Sanskrit concept can emerge from various perspectives” (Malhotra and Babaji, pref.). I grew up in a Srivaishnava family, and the book already gives my tradition a place, noting that in the Sri Vaishnava sampradaya, the practice of bhakti-yoga “involves a seven-fold discipline called sadhana-saptaka” (ch. 8). The entry on dasa is where I felt this most. The authors write that “the true nature (svarupa) of a jiva” is “to be a dasa of Bhagavan” (ch. 10). On this point, my tradition and the book agree completely. What the Srivaishnava tradition adds is how far this idea reaches into ordinary life. In Ramanuja’s Vishishtadvaita, the jiva is understood as the shesha of Bhagavan, one whose very existence is for Him. In Srivaishnava homes, this idea has even crossed from Sanskrit into Tamil, where a person refers to himself not as “I” but as adiyen, “I who am at your feet.” The book itself describes this kind of movement in its introduction, where it explains that “the meta-structure of Sanskrit was transmitted top-down into local languages and, simultaneously, there was a bottom-up assimilation of local culture and language into Sanskrit’s flexible, open architecture” (introd.). Hence, adiyen is a Tamil word that carries a Sanskrit concept, and it carries it so faithfully that the concept became a part of everyday speech. Suppose a reader saw “slave” where a Srivaishnava says adiyen. In that case, the translation would not only be inaccurate, but it would also turn an act of humility into an act of oppression.

The same is true of a word that the book does not yet include, kainkarya, which is, to me, one of the most beautiful words in my tradition. Kainkarya comes from kinkara, the one who asks “kim karomi?”, which means “what shall I do?” In the Srivaishnava understanding, kainkarya means more than doing a task for Bhagavan or for one’s acharya. A person in kainkarya completes the task so quickly and so devotedly that he immediately turns back and asks, “kim karomi?”, what else may I do? English would most likely translate kainkarya as “service,” and yet the book has already shown that this word does not work even for seva, since service is done “often in expectation of some material benefit” (ch. 10). But kainkarya goes even further than seva, as it names not only the act but the eagerness that follows it. Hence, kainkarya illustrates, and even expands upon, the book’s central point. A word this vast and this deep cannot be reduced to a single English word, and any attempt to do so is simply wrong. Entries like this one would gain from the voice of the Vishishtadvaita tradition, and no doubt the same is true of the Madhva, Advaita, Shaiva Siddhanta and Shakta traditions, each of which holds words that English has yet to learn. I saw this most clearly while writing an earlier essay on how Shankaracharya and Ramanujacharya read the Brahma Sutras (Iyengar, “Two Readings”), where the very same sutra carries two complete philosophies depending on the acharya who comments on it.

It was this question, of who decides what a Sanskrit word is allowed to mean, that shaped my choice of where to pursue a PhD. I wanted to work on Sanskrit with computational tools, and to do it in India, where Sanskrit is still a part of daily life. Scholars of Greek and Latin have spent decades measuring their texts, counting how words are used, tracing how their meanings shift, and even identifying authors by the patterns of their meter. Sanskrit, with a far larger body of texts, has received much less of that attention. Contributing to that kind of work, for example by asking whether chandas, the meter of a verse, can act as a fingerprint of the poet who composed it, is what I would love to spend my research life on, and that idea is what led me to IIT Hyderabad, where I received an offer of admission to the PhD program. I declined it for now and told the department that I plan to apply again in a few years, and they supported that decision. Before I begin a PhD, I want to spend time doing exactly this kind of work, which is reading primary sources closely, organizing large amounts of material, and arguing over what each word means. That is also the work this book was built on. The authors also make a hopeful point about where this work can happen. They argue that “the internet and online channels especially in recent times, have helped counter the narrative in popular consciousness,” and that it is “through these informal channels that much of the revival and re-legitimization can happen” (Malhotra and Babaji, introd.). That is one reason I have begun writing in public. When I finished Sanskrit Non-Translatables, I understood that a civilization does not need to be conquered to lose its ideas. It can lose them slowly, one translation at a time, each time someone picks one meaning of a word and drops all the others. And just as my grandfather left my first name open for me to discover, this book leaves me with fifty-four words that are still open, and I hope to spend the next few years helping to keep them that way.
